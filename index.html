Research portfolio · Chemistry & computation

Finding order inside foam.

I built an image-to-graph pipeline for quasi-2D soap foams. It detects every bubble under the microscope, follows each one through time, and tests a 1952 law of foam coarsening against the measurements. It also tests whether the measurements themselves can be trusted.

Key findings

Featured project

Foam GNN: von Neumann's law, measured

A soap foam squeezed between two glass plates coarsens: small bubbles shrink and vanish, large ones grow. Von Neumann's law predicts each bubble's rate from just its number of neighbours, dA/dt = K(n − 6). The project turns microscope time-lapses into a neighbour graph for every frame, then checks whether that rule holds across three different foams, and whether a graph neural network can do better.

22 / 22

Neighbour-swap (T1) events confirmed by hand, one at a time. False-positive rate 0/22, 95% CI [0%, 14.9%].

A negative result, reported as one. Across nine held-out-foam tests, the graph neural network never beat the best simple baseline and was significantly worse in seven. Von Neumann's law was the best model in eight of nine. For this target, the neighbour count n appears to carry all the useful information in the graph.

Five measurement artifacts that each faked a failure

For a while the law appeared to fail, once with K = −1.74. Every failure came from the measurement, not the physics, and each was caught by a check that didn't depend on getting the expected answer.

  1. Propagation ratchet

    Identities carried forward could swallow neighbours and never split back. Foam A fell from 385 to 106 bubbles; an independent count found 219.

  2. Plateau borders counted as bubbles

    Bright liquid channels between films passed as gas and corrupted the neighbour count. Detection F1 rose from 0.515 to 0.899 after a per-region intensity gate.

  3. Foam-mask threshold cliff

    The foam/background mask sat on a step in its own threshold curve, so it flickered from frame to frame and flooded late frames.

  4. Leverage

    Least squares weights each bubble by (n−6)². 1.2% of measurements carried 48% of the fit weight and flipped K's sign.

  5. Training on rejected data

    An apparent "network beats physics" result rested on a foam later rejected for a segmentation defect. It was retracted.
